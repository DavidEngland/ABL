Grid scales ($\Delta z$) play a critical role in modeling atmospheric turbulence because stability diagnostics are highly sensitive to vertical resolution, particularly in the **Stable Boundary Layer (SBL)**. In numerical weather prediction (NWP), turbulence is often calculated using **Bulk Richardson numbers ($Ri_b$)** averaged over a grid layer, but the physical processes are actually governed by **local Gradient Richardson numbers ($Ri_g$)**.

The following points detail how grid scales influence modeling accuracy according to the sources:

*   **The Curvature Bias and Over-mixing:** In the SBL, the local Richardson number ($Ri_g$) typically exhibits a concave-down curvature. When models use coarse grid scales, they average over this curvature, which—due to **Jensen’s inequality**—results in an **underestimation of local stability** ($Ri_b < Ri_g$). This leads models to erroneously maintain turbulence (over-mixing), resulting in "warm biases" at night and incorrect inversion heights.
*   **Grid Sensitivity in Stability Classification:** Coarse grid spacing frequently **misclassifies stability**, failing to capture the point where turbulence collapses. The sources identify that the **Bias Ratio ($B$)**, defined as $Ri_g(z_g) / Ri_b$, is a primary metric for identifying how much a specific grid scale distorts the representation of stability.
*   **Impact on Surface Fluxes:** In the provided Julia "toy model," the grid scale directly dictates the magnitude of temperature and humidity changes in the atmosphere. For example, the **sensible heat flux ($H$)** modifies the first model level's temperature based on the thickness of that specific layer ($dz_1$); a larger grid cell would dilute the surface heating or cooling effect more than a finer one.
*   **Resolution-Aware Corrections:** To fix these errors without requiring prohibitively fine grids, researchers use a **grid-damping factor ($G$)** to correct eddy diffusivities. Machine learning models trained to predict this factor have shown that **grid scale ($\Delta z$)** is one of the two most important features (accounting for 85% of the model's predictive power) in accurately adjusting for these biases.
*   **Performance at Specific Scales:** Corrected schemes have demonstrated a **bias reduction of 40–55%** at grid scales between **60 and 100 meters**, highlighting that even relatively coarse models can be improved if they are made "resolution-aware".

**Analogy**
Modeling atmospheric stability on a coarse grid is like trying to map the elevation of a jagged mountain range using only 10-mile averages. You would completely miss the sharp peaks and deep crevices (the local stability gradients), likely concluding the terrain is much flatter and easier to traverse (more turbulent/mixed) than it actually is.
