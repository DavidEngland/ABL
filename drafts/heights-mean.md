According to the sources, there are two primary heights used to represent the center or representative point for logarithmic wind profiles:

*   **Geometric Mean Height ($z_g$):** Defined as **$z_g = \sqrt{z_0 z_1}$**, this is considered the **"natural center"** for log-like profiles. In the context of the project, it is the height where the point-value of the local Gradient Richardson number ($Ri_g$) is evaluated to identify stability.
*   **Logarithmic Mean Height ($z_L$):** Defined as **$z_L = (z_1 - z_0)/\ln(z_1/z_0)$**, this is the **exact representative height** for the velocity difference ($\Delta U$) specifically within a log wind law.

The sources emphasize the use of the **geometric mean height ($z_g$)** as a key strategy for correcting grid sensitivity and curvature bias in the stable boundary layer.

**Analogy**
If you were trying to find the "average" intensity of a sound that grows exponentially as you move toward a speaker, taking a simple middle point between two distances (the arithmetic mean) would be misleading because the sound changes much faster closer to the source. The **geometric mean height** acts like the specialized "sweet spot" that accounts for that non-linear, logarithmic behavior, ensuring the measurement reflects the true state of the environment rather than just the middle of the physical space.
