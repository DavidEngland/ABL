The Richardson number ($Ri$) acts as a critical, non-dimensional indicator of atmospheric stability by evaluating the ratio of buoyancy to mechanical shear, determining if turbulence is produced, maintained, or suppressed. In the surface layer, $Ri=0$ indicates neutral conditions, $0 < Ri < 0.25$ signifies weak stability, while $Ri < 0$ denotes instability, directly relating to turbulence regimes. [1, 2]
Richardson Number in Turbulence Regimes

• Near-Neutral Regime (): Turbulence is dominated by mechanical shear, with buoyancy playing a minimal role. Turbulence persists, but diminishes as $Ri$ approaches the critical value of $0.25$.
• Free Convection Regime (): Turbulence is dominated by strong vertical motions, heat fluxes, and convection (negative $Ri$). In these highly unstable, weak-wind conditions, convective eddies drive transport, and the $Ri$ accurately reflects convective, rather than shear-driven, turbulence.
• Stable Regime (): Mechanical turbulence is suppressed, with $Ri > 0.25$ often marking the transition to laminar or very weak, intermittent turbulence. [3, 4, 5, 6]

Key Aspects of the Richardson Number

• Definition: Defined as the ratio of buoyancy flux to turbulent production by mean shear, sometimes used as the gradient $Ri$ or flux $R_f$.
• Critical Value (): The critical value for the suppression of turbulence is generally considered to be $0.25$, although some studies suggest $0.2 < Ri_c < 1.0$.
• Bulk Richardson Number (): Used in practical applications, such as numerical weather prediction, to estimate atmospheric stability across a layer, which can be linked to the Monin-Obukhov stability parameter ($\zeta$). [3, 4, 5, 7, 8]

The Richardson number is thus vital for identifying the threshold where shear-driven turbulence ($Ri \approx 0$) gives way to either strong convection ($Ri \ll 0$) or suppression ($Ri > 0.25$). [9]
